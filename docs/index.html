Catch the invoices that shouldn't be paid, and show why.

Ledger Sentinel is an AI agent that screens a company's supplier payment run for duplicate payments, vendor impersonation and phantom vendors. It holds the risky invoices and lays out, for every decision, the exact rules it applied and the records behind them.

Why the obvious rule fails

The first rule anyone writes is "flag an invoice that looks almost identical to one we already paid." But honest suppliers send near-identical invoices every billing cycle: of legitimate invoices are more than 95% text-identical to an earlier one. That rule catches of the fraud.

The signal is timing. A duplicate arrives too early for that vendor's usual billing rhythm, while routine invoices arrive on schedule.

Text similarity can't separate fraud from routine billing; arrival timing catches much of it

The spike at exactly 1× on the right is vendors with too little history to have a rhythm yet, including every phantom vendor. Those are caught by other signals: vendor age, round amounts, missing purchase orders.

Why ship the tree when the forest scores higher?

Holding a supplier's payment has to be justified to that supplier and to an auditor. The decision tree gives at most readable conditions for every decision; the forest averages 300 trees and can't show its work.

The forest's lead is real, not noise: across 2,000 paired bootstrap resamples of the test period, its F1 is higher of the time (difference , 95% interval to ). Shipping the tree is a trade-off made on purpose.

Fairness audit

Bias in past labels carries straight into the model, and hiding the sensitive column only partly fixes it. Models learn from what past investigators wrote down, not from ground truth. In this ledger one region spent years under an enhanced-scrutiny policy, so of its clean invoices carry a fraud label they never earned. Two otherwise identical models were trained on those labels; one could also see the vendor's region.

How it works

What the agent does with each invoice, how the models were built and tested, and what the results can't tell you.

1PerceivePull the invoice and the vendor's history: earlier invoices, bank-detail changes, purchase orders.
2DecideScore it with an interpretable decision tree and recover the exact rule chain it followed.
3ActRelease it into the payment run, hold it for an analyst, or escalate and block payment.
4ExplainIn the full pipeline, Claude turns the rule chain and records into an analyst's case note. It is never asked whether an invoice is fraud.

Method

  • Point-in-time features. Every feature is computed only from records that existed before the invoice arrived, and a test proves no future data leaks in.
  • Split by time. Train, validation and test are consecutive periods; the test period was opened once, at the end.
  • Cost-weighted tuning. A missed fraud is weighted times a false alarm.
  • Honest uncertainty. Paired-bootstrap intervals for model metrics and Wilson intervals for per-region rates.

Limitations

  • Synthetic data. Results show the method recovers planted patterns, not real-world accuracy.
  • Inflated fraud rate (about 5%, far above real ledgers) and a small test set of fraud cases.
  • No adversary. A fraudster who knew the model watched billing rhythm would simply wait.
